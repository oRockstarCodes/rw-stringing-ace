Second Order Differential Equations

Second-Order Differential Equations

Oct 14, 202610 min read

  • concept
  • ele302

A second-order circuit obeys dt2d2x​+2αdtdx​+ω02​x=f. Its solution is a natural part, found from the roots of s2+2αs+ω02​=0, plus a forced part, the value it settles to.

Ch 8 uses this in Steps 2 and 4 without stopping to explain the maths. This page covers why the method works: where the characteristic equation comes from, why there are three cases, how complex roots turn into a decaying cosine, and how to find the constants (including the phase-angle quadrant trap).

Intuition

What the equation says

In standard form,

dt2d2x​+2αdtdx​+ω02​x=f
  • x is the voltage or current you’re solving for.
  • ω02​x is the “restoring” term: the further x is from 0, the harder it’s pulled back, like a spring. On its own (with α=0) it makes x oscillate at ω0​ (see LC Oscillation).
  • 2αdtdx​ is the “damping” term: it resists change, like friction, and comes from the resistance.
  • f is the forcing: whatever the sources are pushing. It’s 0 if there’s no source after switching, and a constant for a DC source.

In a real circuit α and ω02​ are always positive, which is why every response eventually dies out or settles.

Natural + forced

The equation is linear: x and its derivatives only appear on their own, never squared or multiplied together. That means solutions can be added. So split the answer into two parts:

  • The natural solution xN​ solves the equation with f=0 (the homogeneous equation). It’s how the circuit behaves on its own, and it contains the two unknown constants.
  • The forced solution xF​ is any one solution of the full equation. For a constant f, the simplest one is a constant: where the circuit settles.

Adding them, xN​+xF​ still satisfies the full equation (the xN​ part contributes 0 to the left side, and the xF​ part contributes f). The two constants in xN​ are then chosen to match the two initial conditions.

Why try est

We need a function whose second derivative, first derivative and value can add up to zero for all time. The exponential is the natural guess, because differentiating it just multiplies it by a constant:

x=Aest⇒dtdx​=sAest,dt2d2x​=s2Aest

Substituting into the homogeneous equation, every term has Aest in common:

(s2+2αs+ω02​)Aest=0

Aest is never zero (unless A=0, which would mean no response at all), so the bracket must be zero. That’s the characteristic equation:

s2+2αs+ω02​=0

It turns a calculus problem into a quadratic. Its roots, from the quadratic formula, are

s1,2​=2−2α±4α2−4ω02​​​=−α±α2−ω02​​

The sign of what’s under the square root (the discriminant) gives the three cases.

Figure 1: Where the roots sit in the complex plane (σ is the real part, jω the imaginary part) for each case. Roots further left decay faster; roots further from the horizontal axis oscillate faster.

Case 1: two real roots (overdamped, α>ω0​)

The square root is real, so s1​ and s2​ are two different real numbers. Both are negative (since α2−ω02​​<α), so both exponentials decay. Each root gives a solution, and because the equation is linear their sum is also a solution:

xN​=A1​es1​t+A2​es2​t

The root closer to zero decays more slowly, so it’s the one that dominates later on.

Case 2: one repeated root (critically damped, α=ω0​)

The square root is zero, so both roots equal −α. Writing A1​e−αt+A2​e−αt doesn’t work: that’s just (A1​+A2​)e−αt, a single constant, and you need two to match two initial conditions. The second solution turns out to be te−αt:

xN​=A1​e−αt+A2​te−αt

You can check that te−αt works. Its derivatives are (1−αt)e−αt and (α2t−2α)e−αt. Substituting with ω02​=α2:

(α2t−2α)+2α(1−αt)+α2t=0✓

Case 3: complex roots (underdamped, α<ω0​)

The square root is of a negative number, so the roots are complex:

s=−α±jωd​,ωd​=ω02​−α2​

What does est mean when s is complex? Split it up and use Euler’s formula, ejθ=cosθ+jsinθ:

e(−α+jωd​)t=e−αtejωd​t=e−αt(cosωd​t+jsinωd​t)

So the real part of s (−α) gives a decaying envelope, and the imaginary part (ωd​) gives an oscillation. A real voltage or current can’t be complex. Combining the two roots (they’re a conjugate pair) cancels the imaginary parts and leaves a real decaying sinusoid, which can be written two equivalent ways:

xN​=Ae−αtcos(ωd​t+θ)=e−αt(B1​cosωd​t+B2​sinωd​t)

The first form (amplitude A and phase θ) is the one used in Ch 8. The second is the textbook’s. Expanding cos(ωd​t+θ)=cosωd​tcosθ−sinωd​tsinθ shows how they’re related:

B1​=Acosθ,B2​=−Asinθ,A=B12​+B22​​

Note that the oscillation is at ωd​, slightly slower than ω0​. Damping slows the swing down as well as shrinking it.

The forced solution

For a constant forcing f (any DC source), try a constant xF​. Its derivatives are 0, so the equation becomes

ω02​xF​=f⇒xF​=ω02​f​

This is the same value you’d get from DC analysis of the circuit as t→∞ (L shorted, C open), which is usually the easier way to find it. Getting it both ways is a good check.

Finding the constants

The total solution is x=xN​+xF​. You need two equations for the two constants, and they come from x(0) and dtdx​(0). Differentiate each form and set t=0 (where e0=1):

Casex(0)dtdx​(0)
OverdampedA1​+A2​+xF​s1​A1​+s2​A2​
Critically dampedA1​+xF​−αA1​+A2​
UnderdampedAcosθ+xF​−αAcosθ−ωd​Asinθ

For the underdamped row, the derivative comes from the product rule:

dtd​[Ae−αtcos(ωd​t+θ)]=Ae−αt[−αcos(ωd​t+θ)−ωd​sin(ωd​t+θ)]

Getting θ in the right quadrant

In the underdamped case you end up knowing Acosθ and Asinθ (call them X and Y). Then A=X2+Y2​, and θ is the angle of the point (X,Y) measured from the positive horizontal axis, in radians.

The trap: a calculator’s tan−1(XY​) only returns angles between −2π​ and 2π​, because XY​ can’t tell (+,+) from (−,−), or (+,−) from (−,+). Fix it using the signs:

Sign of X=AcosθSign of Y=AsinθQuadrantθ

(Adding or subtracting 2π gives the same angle, so +π and −π both work in the 2nd and 3rd quadrants; the table just keeps θ between −π and π.) Always finish by checking that Acosθ and Asinθ come back with the right signs.

Key formulas

Standard form and characteristic equation

dt2d2x​+2αdtdx​+ω02​x=f,s2+2αs+ω02​=0,s1,2​=−α±α2−ω02​​

Natural solution

xN​=⎩⎨⎧​A1​es1​t+A2​es2​t(A1​+A2​t)e−αtAe−αtcos(ωd​t+θ)​α>ω0​α=ω0​α<ω0​​ωd​=ω02​−α2​

Forced and total solution (constant forcing)

xF​=ω02​f​,x=xN​+xF​

Converting between the underdamped forms

B1​=Acosθ,B2​=−Asinθ,A=B12​+B22​​
  • x: the voltage or current being solved for; f: the forcing (right-hand side)
  • α: neper frequency (Np/s); ω0​: resonant frequency (rad/s); ωd​: damped frequency (rad/s)
  • s1​,s2​: roots of the characteristic equation
  • A1​,A2​,A,θ,B1​,B2​: constants set by the initial conditions; θ in radians

Worked examples

Example 1: Overdamped with a constant forcing

Solve dt2d2x​+7dtdx​+10x=20 with x(0)=0 and dtdx​(0)=0.

  1. Roots: s2+7s+10=0 factors as (s+2)(s+5)=0, so s=−2,−5. Two real roots: overdamped.
  2. Forced: xF​=1020​=2.
  3. Total: x=2+A1​e−2t+A2​e−5t.
  4. Constants (from the table): x(0)=2+A1​+A2​=0,dtdx​(0)=−2A1​−5A2​=0 The second gives A1​=−2.5A2​. Substituting: −1.5A2​=−2, so A2​=34​ and A1​=−310​. x(t)=2−310​e−2t+34​e−5t

Example 2: Critically damped

Solve dt2d2x​+4dtdx​+4x=0 with x(0)=3 and dtdx​(0)=0.

  1. Roots: s2+4s+4=(s+2)2=0, so s=−2 twice. Critically damped, α=2.
  2. Forced: f=0, so xF​=0, and x=(A1​+A2​t)e−2t.
  3. Constants: x(0)=A1​=3. dtdx​(0)=−2A1​+A2​=0, so A2​=6. x(t)=(3+6t)e−2t

Example 3: Underdamped, with a third-quadrant angle

Solve dt2d2x​+2dtdx​+5x=0 with x(0)=−1 and dtdx​(0)=5.

  1. Roots: s=−1±1−5​=−1±j2. Underdamped, α=1, ωd​=2.
  2. Forced: xF​=0, so x=Ae−tcos(2t+θ).
  3. Constants:
    • Acosθ=x(0)−xF​=−1
    • −αAcosθ−ωd​Asinθ=5: −1(−1)−2Asinθ=5, so Asinθ=−2
  4. Amplitude: A=(−1)2+(−2)2​=5​=2.236
  5. Angle. Both X=−1 and Y=−2 are negative: third quadrant. The calculator gives tan−1(−1−2​)=tan−1(2)=1.107, which is in the first quadrant (both positive), so it’s wrong. Subtract π: θ=1.107−π=−2.034 rad Check: 5​cos(−2.034)=−1 ✓ and 5​sin(−2.034)=−2 ✓. x(t)=2.236e−tcos(2t−2.034)
  6. In the textbook’s form: B1​=Acosθ=−1 and B2​=−Asinθ=2, so x(t)=e−t(−cos2t+2sin2t). Same function.

Common mistakes

  • Applying the initial conditions to xN​ alone and forgetting xF​
  • Writing A1​e−αt+A2​e−αt for a repeated root. The second term needs a t
  • Using ω0​ instead of ωd​ inside the cosine
  • Differentiating Ae−αtcos(ωd​t+θ) without the product rule, and losing the −α term
  • Trusting tan−1 when Acosθ is negative
  • Leaving θ in degrees while ωd​t is in radians
