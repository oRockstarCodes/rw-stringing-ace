Damping in Series and Parallel RLC

Damping in Series and Parallel RLC

Oct 14, 20267 min read

  • concept
  • ele302

Damping is how quickly a resistor drains away the energy swinging between L and C. In a series RLC, a bigger R means more damping; in a parallel RLC, a bigger R means less.

In Ch 8 this shows up as two different formulas for the neper frequency α, 2LR​ for series and 2RC1​ for parallel, which go in opposite directions as R changes. This page explains why, so the formulas aren’t just something to memorize.

Intuition

What damping measures

Without resistance, an L and a C pass energy back and forth forever (see LC Oscillation). A resistor turns some of that energy into heat on every swing. The neper frequency α (in Np/s) measures how fast that happens, and comparing it with the resonant frequency ω0​=LC​1​ decides the shape of the response:

ComparisonCaseWhat it looks like
α<ω0​UnderdampedOscillates inside a decaying envelope
α=ω0​Critically dampedFastest return to zero without overshooting
α>ω0​OverdampedCreeps back to zero, no oscillation

So “more damping” means a bigger α, which pushes the circuit toward overdamped. The question is how R affects α, and that depends on how much power the resistor burns, which depends on how it’s connected.

Series: the resistor is in the path of the current

Figure 1: Series RLC. The current swinging between L and C has to pass through R.

In a series circuit, the current sloshing between L and C has to flow through the resistor. The power it burns is

P=i2R

For the same current, a bigger R burns more power, so the energy drains away faster and the oscillation dies sooner. Look at the two extremes:

  • R=0: the resistor is just a wire. Nothing is burned, and the circuit rings forever (pure LC).
  • R very large: the resistor chokes the current so much that the capacitor can barely discharge, let alone swing past zero. Heavily overdamped.

That’s why the series formula grows with R:

α=2LR​

The L underneath makes sense too: a bigger inductor carries more “inertia” (stores more energy for the same current), so the same resistor takes longer to drain it.

Parallel: the resistor sits beside the oscillation

Figure 2: Parallel RLC. R has the same voltage as L and C, and only the current that leaks through it is lost.

In a parallel circuit, the energy swings between L and C directly, and the resistor sits beside them with the same voltage v. Only the current that leaks through it, iR​=Rv​, is lost. The power it burns is

P=Rv2​

Now a bigger R lets less current leak away, so less energy is burned and the oscillation lasts longer. The extremes are flipped:

  • R→∞: the resistor is an open circuit, as if it weren’t there. Nothing leaks, and the circuit rings forever.
  • R very small: the resistor is nearly a short across the LC, which kills the voltage almost immediately. Heavily overdamped.

That’s why the parallel formula shrinks as R grows:

α=2RC1​

Same parts, connected both ways

Take L=1 H and C=41​ F, so ω0​=1/4​1​=2 rad/s, and try two resistors:

R=1 ΩR=10 Ω
Series, α=2LR​α=0.5: underdampedα=5: overdamped
Parallel, α=2RC1​α=2: critically dampedα=0.2: underdamped

Going from 1 Ω to 10 Ω pushes the series circuit toward overdamped and the parallel circuit away from it.

The critical resistance

Setting α=ω0​ gives the resistance that makes each circuit exactly critically damped:

Series: 2LR​=LC​1​⇒Rc​=2CL​​,Parallel: 2RC1​=LC​1​⇒Rc​=21​CL​​

With the parts above, L/C​=2, so Rc​=4 Ω in series and 1 Ω in parallel (which is why the parallel 1 Ω entry in the table is critically damped). Then:

CircuitUnderdampedCritically dampedOverdamped
SeriesR<Rc​R=Rc​R>Rc​
ParallelR>Rc​R=Rc​R<Rc​

Circuits that are neither

The two formulas only apply to a pure series or pure parallel RLC: one R, one L and one C, all in series or all in parallel (after turning off the sources). Many circuits are neither. For example, in Ch 8 Example 11, L is in parallel with C, and the resistor is in series with that pair. Neither formula obviously fits.

When that happens, don’t guess. Derive the circuit’s own differential equation with KVL/KCL, put it in standard form, and read α off it:

dt2d2x​+read α here2α​​dtdx​+ω02​x=f

In Example 11 that gives α=2RC1​: the parallel formula, even though the resistor is in series. Only the derivation tells you that. (It makes sense physically: the L and C swap energy directly with each other, and the resistor only sees the current going in and out of the pair.)

Source transformations can often turn an awkward circuit into a pure series or parallel one first; see Source Transformation.

Key formulas

Neper frequency

α=2LR​(series),α=2RC1​(parallel)

Resonant frequency (same for both)

ω0​=LC​1​

Critical resistance

Rc​=2CL​​(series),Rc​=21​CL​​(parallel)

Power burned in the resistor

P=i2R(series: same current as L and C),P=Rv2​(parallel: same voltage as L and C)
  • α: neper frequency, how fast the response decays (Np/s)
  • ω0​: resonant frequency (rad/s)
  • Rc​: the resistance that makes the circuit critically damped
  • R, L, C: the resistance, inductance and capacitance with the sources turned off

Worked example

Example 1: Same parts, two connections

An inductor L=0.5 H and a capacitor C=2 mF are connected with a resistor R. (a) Find the critical resistance for a series connection and for a parallel connection. (b) Classify the response for R=20 Ω and R=5 Ω, both ways.

(a) First ω0​=(0.5)(0.002)​1​=0.001​1​=31.62 rad/s, and L/C​=250​=15.81.

  • Series: Rc​=2(15.81)=31.62 Ω
  • Parallel: Rc​=21​(15.81)=7.91 Ω

(b)

RSeries α=2LR​ResultParallel α=2RC1​Result
20 Ω120​=2020<31.62: underdamped2(20)(0.002)1​=12.512.5<31.62: underdamped
5 Ω15​=55<31.62: underdamped2(5)(0.002)1​=5050>31.62: overdamped

Shrinking R from 20 Ω to 5 Ω made the series circuit less damped (α fell from 20 to 5) but the parallel circuit more damped (α rose from 12.5 to 50), enough to tip it into overdamped. You can read the same thing off part (a): 5 Ω is below the parallel Rc​ of 7.91 Ω, and in parallel a resistance below Rc​ means overdamped.

Common mistakes

  • Using α=2LR​ on a parallel circuit, or 2RC1​ on a series one
  • Assuming “more resistance always means more damping”. That’s only true in series
  • Forcing one of the two formulas onto a circuit that’s neither pure series nor pure parallel, instead of deriving its equation
  • Finding R with the sources still connected. α comes from the circuit with independent sources turned off
