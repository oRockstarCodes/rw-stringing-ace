Capacitors and Inductors

Capacitors and Inductors

Oct 14, 202613 min read

  • concept
  • ele302

Capacitors and inductors are the two energy-storage elements: a capacitor stores energy in an electric field set up by its voltage, and an inductor stores energy in a magnetic field set up by its current.

Resistors only turn energy into heat. Capacitors and inductors store it and give it back later, which is why circuits with them have a history: what they do now depends on what happened before. Everything in Ch 8 comes from the two element laws on this page.

Intuition

The capacitor

A capacitor is two conductors (plates) separated by an insulator called the dielectric (Figure 1, left). Push current into one terminal and charge +q builds up on that plate, while the same amount leaves the other plate, leaving it at −q. No charge actually crosses the gap. The separated charge sets up an electric field E between the plates, and that field is where the energy is stored.

The more charge on the plates, the bigger the voltage between them. The ratio is the capacitance:

q=Cv,C=dεA​
  • q: charge on the plates (C)
  • C: capacitance, in farads (F)
  • ε: permittivity of the dielectric
  • A: plate area; d: gap between the plates

Bigger plates or a smaller gap hold more charge per volt. Real values range from picofarads (pF) to millifarads (mF); 1 F is huge.

Where i=Cdtdv​ comes from. Current is the rate charge arrives, i=dtdq​. Differentiate q=Cv:

iC​=CdtdvC​​

So a capacitor’s current depends on how fast its voltage changes, not on the voltage itself. A capacitor sitting at a steady 100 V carries no current at all. To change its voltage quickly you need a large current, and to change it instantly you’d need an infinite one.

Water analogy: a capacitor is like a tank with a stretchy rubber membrane across the middle. Water (charge) pushed in one side bulges the membrane and pushes the same amount out the other side, so it looks like water flows "through", even though none crosses. The bulge (voltage) builds up gradually and pushes back.

The inductor

An inductor is a coil of wire (Figure 1, right). Current through the coil sets up a magnetic field B through its middle, and that field is where the energy is stored. The total magnetic flux linking the coil, λ, is proportional to the current. The ratio is the inductance:

λ=Li,L=ℓN2μA​
  • λ: flux linkage (Wb·turns)
  • L: inductance, in henries (H)
  • N: number of turns; μ: permeability of the core
  • A: cross-sectional area of the coil; ℓ: its length

More turns, a bigger coil or an iron core all give more inductance. Real values range from microhenries (µH) to a few henries.

Where v=Ldtdi​ comes from. Faraday’s law says a changing flux induces a voltage, v=dtdλ​. Differentiate λ=Li:

vL​=LdtdiL​​

So an inductor’s voltage depends on how fast its current changes. A steady current, however large, produces no voltage. By Lenz’s law the induced voltage always opposes the change that caused it: try to speed the current up and the inductor pushes back, try to stop it and the inductor pushes forward to keep it going. This is the inductor’s “inertia”, the reason an LC circuit overshoots and rings.

Figure 1: A capacitor stores energy in the electric field between its plates (left). An inductor stores energy in the magnetic field through its coil (right).

Passive sign convention

Both laws assume the passive sign convention: the current arrow points into the + terminal of the voltage. If your current arrow points into the − terminal instead, put a minus sign in front: iC​=−CdtdvC​​, vL​=−LdtdiL​​.

Most sign errors in Ch 8 slopes come from this. Before writing dtdvC​​=CiC​​, check that iC​ is the current flowing into the capacitor's + terminal.

Integral form

Integrating the element law from a starting time t0​ gives the value at any later time:

vC​(t)=C1​∫t0​t​iC​dt+vC​(t0​),iL​(t)=L1​∫t0​t​vL​dt+iL​(t0​)

The integral adds up everything that has happened since t0​, and the last term is where it started. That’s the “memory”: a capacitor’s voltage now depends on all the current that has ever flowed into it, not just the current right now. This is also why you need initial conditions to solve any circuit with C or L in it.

Stored energy

The power into any element is p=vi. For a capacitor, substitute i=Cdtdv​ and integrate from an uncharged state (v=0):

wC​=∫pdt=∫Cvdtdv​dt=∫0v​Cvdv=21​Cv2

The inductor works the same way with v=Ldtdi​:

wL​=∫Lidtdi​dt=∫0i​Lidi=21​Li2

Three things follow from these:

  • The energy is never negative (it’s a square), so an ideal C or L can only give back energy it took in earlier. It can’t generate any.
  • Ideal capacitors and inductors don’t dissipate. If p is positive the element is absorbing and storing energy; if negative, it’s releasing what it stored. Over a full cycle of a steady AC signal, they absorb and release the same amount.
  • The energy depends only on vC​ (or iL​). That’s the link to continuity, next.

Why vC​ and iL​ can’t jump

Suppose a capacitor’s voltage jumped from v1​ to v2​ in zero time. Then:

  • dtdv​=0v2​−v1​​ is infinite, so iC​=Cdtdv​ would be infinite.
  • Equivalently, the stored energy would change from 21​Cv12​ to 21​Cv22​ in zero time, which needs infinite power.

Neither is possible in a real circuit, so capacitor voltage is continuous. The same argument with vL​=Ldtdi​ shows inductor current is continuous. At a switching instant t=0:

vC​(0+)=vC​(0−),iL​(0+)=iL​(0−)

The other variable has no such protection. A capacitor’s current can jump instantly (it only sets the slope of the voltage), and so can an inductor’s voltage. In a graph, a jump in iC​ shows up as a sudden change of slope (a corner) in vC​, never a step (see Example 1 below).

At t=0+ you can replace a capacitor with a voltage source of value vC​(0−), and an inductor with a current source of value iL​(0−). Ch 8 Step 3 uses exactly this.

DC steady state

When a DC circuit has been on for a long time, every voltage and current has stopped changing, so every derivative is 0:

  • Capacitor: iC​=C⋅0=0. No current flows, so it acts like an open circuit. It still holds whatever voltage it charged to.
  • Inductor: vL​=L⋅0=0. No voltage across it, so it acts like a short circuit. It still carries whatever current is flowing.

These are the two rules for finding initial values (the circuit before switching) and final values (as t→∞) in Ch 8.

There’s a matching pair of rules for the instant a source is first switched on, if nothing was stored beforehand: an uncharged capacitor (vC​=0) acts like a short at that instant, and an inductor with no current (iL​=0) acts like an open. They then gradually change toward their DC steady-state behaviour.

Series and parallel

Capacitors combine like resistors the other way round; inductors combine exactly like resistors.

Capacitors in parallel add. Every capacitor has the same voltage v, and the currents add:

i=C1​dtdv​+C2​dtdv​+⋯=(C1​+C2​+⋯)dtdv​⇒Ceq​=C1​+C2​+⋯

Physically, parallel capacitors are like one capacitor with a bigger plate area.

Capacitors in series combine like parallel resistors. They all carry the same current, so they all gain the same charge, and the voltages add: v=C1​q​+C2​q​+⋯, which gives Ceq​1​=C1​1​+C2​1​+⋯. For two, Ceq​=C1​+C2​C1​C2​​. Physically, it’s like one capacitor with a wider gap.

Inductors in series add, because they carry the same current and their voltages add: v=(L1​+L2​+⋯)dtdi​. Inductors in parallel combine like parallel resistors.

Memory aid: L behaves like R. C behaves like R1​ (a conductance), so it flips.

Impedance: how they behave at different frequencies

For a sinusoid at angular frequency ω, dtd​ becomes multiplication by jω, so each element acts like a frequency-dependent resistance called impedance:

ZC​=jωC1​,ZL​=jωL
FrequencyCapacitorInductor
DC (ω=0)ZC​→∞: openZL​=0: short
Very high (ω→∞)ZC​→0: shortZL​→∞: open

This matches the DC steady-state rules above, and it’s also why a sudden change (which contains very high frequencies) gets through a capacitor but is blocked by an inductor. It’s the basis of filters in Ch 14.

Mechanical analogy

ElectricalMechanicalWhy
CapacitorSpringStores energy in how far it’s “stretched” (its voltage)
InductorMassStores energy in how fast it’s “moving” (its current), and resists changes in it (inertia)
ResistorFrictionTurns energy into heat

A charged capacitor connected to an inductor behaves like a stretched spring with a mass on it: it oscillates, at ω0​=LC​1​. Adding resistance (friction) makes the oscillation die out. That’s the whole story of Ch 8.

Real components

Real parts are close to ideal, but not quite:

  • Capacitors leak a little: a very large resistance in parallel lets the charge slowly drain away. They also have a small series resistance (ESR) and a maximum voltage rating. Exceeding it breaks down the dielectric. Electrolytic capacitors are polarized and must be connected the right way round.
  • Inductors are made of wire, so they have a winding resistance in series. At DC a real inductor is a small resistor, not a perfect short. Inductors with iron cores also saturate at high current, where L drops.

In ELE 302 problems, assume ideal elements unless the question says otherwise.

Worked example

Example 1: Capacitor voltage from its current

A 0.5 F capacitor starts at v(0)=2 V. Its current is 1 A for 0<t<2 s, then −0.5 A for 2<t<4 s, then 0. Find v(t) and the stored energy at t=0, 2 and 4 s.

  1. 0<t<2: integral form, with C1​=2: v(t)=2∫0t​1dt+2=2+2t⇒v(2)=6 V
  2. 2<t<4: start from v(2)=6 (the voltage can’t jump, even though the current does): v(t)=2∫2t​(−0.5)dt+6=6−(t−2)⇒v(4)=4 V
  3. t>4: no current, so the voltage just holds: v=4 V.
  4. Energy, w=21​Cv2=41​v2: w(0)=1 J,w(2)=9 J,w(4)=4 J

Figure 2: Example 1. The current jumps at t=2 and t=4, but the voltage only changes slope.

From 0 to 2 s the capacitor absorbs 9−1=8 J. From 2 to 4 s the current reverses and it gives 9−4=5 J back to the circuit. After 4 s it keeps the remaining 4 J indefinitely (an ideal capacitor doesn’t leak).

Example 2: Inductor voltage from its current

The current in a 0.2 H inductor is 0 for t<0 and i(t)=5(1−e−10t) A for t≥0. Find v(t), and the energy stored as t→∞.

  1. Check continuity: i(0+)=5(1−1)=0=i(0−) ✓
  2. Element law: v(t)=Ldtdi​=0.2⋅5⋅10e−10t=10e−10t V(t>0) The voltage jumps from 0 to 10 V at t=0. That’s allowed: only the inductor’s current has to be continuous.
  3. As t→∞: v→0 (the inductor becomes a short, as at DC steady state) and i→5 A, so wL​=21​(0.2)(5)2=2.5 J

Example 3: Combining elements

(a) A 6 μF and a 3 μF capacitor in series, with that pair in parallel with a 4 μF. (b) A 4 H and a 12 H inductor in parallel, with that pair in series with a 2 H.

(a) Series first: 6+36⋅3​=2 μF. Then parallel adds: Ceq​=2+4=6 μF.

(b) Parallel first: 4+124⋅12​=3 H. Then series adds: Leq​=3+2=5 H.

Common mistakes

  • Thinking capacitor current or inductor voltage must be continuous. Only vC​ and iL​ are
  • Mixing up the combination rules: capacitors in parallel add, inductors in series add
  • Forgetting the initial value (vC​(t0​) or iL​(t0​)) when using the integral form
  • Ignoring the passive sign convention, which flips the sign of i=Cdtdv​ or v=Ldtdi​
  • Treating a capacitor at DC steady state as having zero voltage. It has zero current; it can still hold a voltage
  • Thinking a large voltage on a capacitor means a large current. The current depends on how fast the voltage changes
