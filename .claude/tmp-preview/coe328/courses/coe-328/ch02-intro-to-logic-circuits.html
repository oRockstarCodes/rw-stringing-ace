Ch 2: Introduction to Logic Circuits

Overview

Readings Brown & Vranesic §2.1–2.10 · Lectures 2–4 (Logic Gates, Boolean Algebra, NAND and NOR Logic Networks) · weeks 2–4

Logic gates are the building blocks of every digital circuit, and Boolean algebra is the math that describes them. This chapter covers each gate, how to read a circuit as a Boolean expression (and back), the rules for simplifying expressions, the two standard forms (SOP and POS), how to build anything out of just NAND or just NOR gates, and the basic design process: specification → truth table → expression → circuit.

Notes

The basic gates

Every gate has one output and (except NOT) two or more inputs. A gate with n inputs has 2n input combinations, so its truth table has 2n rows.

Notes on each gate

  • NOT: also called complementation. The small circle (bubble) on its output is the negation indicator: anywhere a bubble appears, the signal is inverted.
  • AND: called logical multiplication (A⋅B or just AB). Useful as an enable gate: with one input at 1 the other passes through, with one input at 0 the output is stuck at 0.
  • OR: called logical addition. Unlike binary addition there’s no carry: 1+1=1.
  • NAND / NOR: the universal gates. Either one alone can build NOT, AND, and OR, so it can build any circuit (see NAND and NOR logic).
  • XOR: exactly two inputs. Its output is the sum bit of adding two bits (1+1=10, sum 0), so it’s a 1-bit modulo-2 adder, and it detects when two signals disagree.
  • XNOR: the opposite of XOR, so it’s a 1-bit equality detector.

Active-HIGH and active-LOW

The bubble also tells you what level is active (asserted):

  • No bubble: the input/output is active-HIGH: it “does its thing” when it’s 1.
  • Bubble: the input/output is active-LOW: it “does its thing” when it’s 0.

This leads to a second way to read NAND and NOR gates:

Figure 2.1: The NAND gate is also a negative-OR gate, and the NOR gate is also a negative-AND gate.

  • NAND as negative-OR: output is 1 when A is 0 OR B is 0. Use this view when you’re detecting “any input LOW”.
  • NOR as negative-AND: output is 1 when A is 0 AND B is 0. Use this view when you’re detecting “all inputs LOW”.

Both are the same physical gate; DeMorgan’s theorems (DeMorgan’s theorems) prove the two symbols are equal.

Timing diagrams

In real circuits the inputs are waveforms, not fixed levels. A timing diagram lines up the waveforms on the same time axis. To find a gate’s output waveform:

  1. Draw a vertical line at every edge of every input.
  2. In each interval between lines, the inputs are constant, so apply the truth table.
  3. Draw the output level for each interval.

Try it: click any time slot of A or B to flip it and watch the output. Switch gates with the buttons.

Boolean algebra basics

  • A variable (A, B, …) is 0 or 1. Its complement A (“not A”, “A bar”, or A′) is the opposite value. A variable or its complement is a literal.
  • A sum term is literals ORed together (A+B+C). It’s 0 only when every literal is 0.
  • A product term is literals ANDed together (ABC). It’s 1 only when every literal is 1.

DeMorgan’s theorems

XY=X+YX+Y​=XY

In words: break the bar, change the sign. Splitting a bar over a product turns the AND into an OR, and splitting a bar over a sum turns the OR into an AND. They work for any number of variables:

WXYZ=W+X+Y+ZW+X+Y+Z​=WXYZ

Procedure for a big expression (e.g. A+BC​+D(E+F​)​):

  1. Treat each term under the longest bar as a single variable (X=A+BC​, Y=D(E+F​)).
  2. Apply DeMorgan to the longest bar: X+Y​=XY.
  3. Cancel any double bars (Rule 9).
  4. Repeat on the remaining bars until every bar sits over a single variable.
A+BC​+D(E+F​)​=(A+BC)(D+E+F)

From circuit to expression to truth table

Circuit → expression: start at the inputs and write the expression at each gate output, working toward the final output.

Expression → truth table:

  1. List all 2n input combinations (count in binary).
  2. Find which combinations make the expression 1 (or work out every intermediate column).
  3. Fill in the output column.

For example, A(B+CD) is 1 only when A=1 and B+CD=1, i.e. when A=1,B=1 (any C,D), or A=1,C=1,D=1 (any B).

Simplifying with Boolean algebra

A simpler expression means fewer gates for the same function: fewer chips, less power, and less material to make them. The usual moves:

  • Factor out a common literal: AB+AB=A(B+B)=A
  • Absorb with Rule 10: A+AB=A
  • Remove a complemented literal with Rule 11: A+AB=A+B
  • Duplicate a term you need twice (Rule 5: X=X+X)

Lecture example: AB+A(B+C)+B(B+C)

​=AB+AB+AC+BB+BC=AB+AC+B+BC=AB+AC+B=B+AC​​distributeRules 5, 7Rule 10 (B+BC=B)Rule 10 (B+AB=B)​

Five gates become two (one AND, one OR).

Algebra works, but it’s easy to miss a step. Ch 4 gives a systematic method (the Karnaugh map).

Standard forms: SOP and POS

Any expression can be rewritten in one of two standard forms:

FormLooks likeBuilt with
Sum of products (SOP)AB+AC+BCDAND gates feeding one OR gate (AND-OR)
Product of sums (POS)(A+B)(A+C)(B+C+D)OR gates feeding one AND gate (OR-AND)

In both forms a bar can only cover one variable: AB is allowed, AB is not.

  • To get SOP from a general expression, distribute: A(B+CD)=AB+ACD.
  • The domain is the set of all variables in the expression. A standard (canonical) SOP or POS has every variable of the domain in every term.

Converting to standard SOP: multiply each product term by (X+X) for each missing variable X (that’s multiplying by 1), and expand. Each missing variable doubles the number of terms.

AB(domain A,B,C)=AB(C+C)=ABC+ABC

Converting to standard POS: add XX for each missing variable (that’s adding 0), then use Rule 12 to split.

A+B=A+B+CC=(A+B+C)(A+B+C)

Minterms and maxterms

Each row of a truth table has one standard product term that’s 1 only on that row, and one standard sum term that’s 0 only on that row:

  • Minterm mi​: product term that’s 1 only on row i. A 1 in the row becomes the variable, a 0 becomes its complement.
  • Maxterm Mi​: sum term that’s 0 only on row i. A 0 in the row becomes the variable, a 1 becomes its complement.
Row iABCMinterm mi​Maxterm Mi​
00 0 0ABCA+B+C
10 0 1ABCA+B+C

61 1 0ABCA+B+C
71 1 1ABCA+B+C

So from any truth table:

  • Standard SOP = OR of the minterms for the rows where the output is 1. Shorthand: f=∑m(…).
  • Standard POS = AND of the maxterms for the rows where the output is 0. Shorthand: f=∏M(…).

The two lists are complements of each other: every row number is in exactly one of them.

The lecture slides (Floyd) describe this as the "binary value" of a term: ABCD has binary value 1010 because that's the one input that makes it 1, and A+B+C+D has binary value 0101 because that's the one input that makes it 0. Same idea as m10​ and M5​.

NAND and NOR logic

NAND and NOR are universal: each one alone can make every other gate.

To getWith NAND onlyWith NOR only
NOTtie the inputs together: AA=Atie the inputs together: A+A​=A
ANDNAND, then a NAND inverterinvert both inputs, then NOR: A+B​=AB
ORinvert both inputs, then NAND: AB=A+BNOR, then a NOR inverter
NOROR (above), then a NAND inverter—
NAND—AND (above), then a NOR inverter

Two-level NAND = SOP. An AND-OR circuit becomes NAND-NAND just by swapping every gate for a NAND, because the bubbles cancel in pairs:

X=ABCD=AB+CD=AB+CD

Two-level NOR = POS. In the same way, an OR-AND circuit becomes NOR-NOR:

X=A+B​+C+D​​=(A+B)(C+D)

Dual symbols. To read a NAND or NOR circuit quickly, redraw it so every connection is bubble-to-bubble or plain-to-plain. Bubbles that meet cancel (they’re a double inversion), so you can read the gates as plain AND/OR:

  • In NAND logic, draw gates as NAND and negative-OR symbols, alternating by level, with the output gate as a negative-OR.
  • In NOR logic, draw gates as NOR and negative-AND symbols, with the output gate as a negative-AND.
  • If a bubble meets a plain input, that variable picks up a bar.

Design process

The textbook’s three steps (§2.8):

  1. Specify exactly what the circuit should do.
  2. Synthesize: specification → truth table → Boolean expression → circuit.
  3. Test: apply every input combination and check the output.

Design example: three-way light control. A room has three doors, each with a switch (x1​, x2​, x3​) for the same light f. Flipping any one switch must change the light. Start with the light off when all switches are open (000). Then flipping one switch turns it on, flipping a second turns it off, and so on: the light is on when an odd number of switches are closed.

Both are correct implementations (NOT → AND → OR for SOP, NOT → OR → AND for POS). This function is also just f=x1​⊕x2​⊕x3​.

Example 2: Choosing a gate from active-LOW sensors

Like Lecture 2, Examples 10–11 and 14–15

A press has two safety guards. Each guard’s sensor outputs LOW when the guard is closed and HIGH when it’s open. (a) Pick one gate whose output is HIGH (enabling the press) only when both guards are closed. (b) Pick one gate whose output is HIGH (a warning light) when at least one guard is open.

(a) “Both inputs LOW → output HIGH” is a negative-AND, which is a NOR gate: X=A+B​=AB.

(b) “At least one input HIGH → output HIGH” is a plain OR gate: X=A+B.

When a question describes inputs that are active-LOW, think in terms of negative-OR (NAND) and negative-AND (NOR).

Example 3: DeMorgan with several variables

Like Lecture 3, Examples 3–5

Apply DeMorgan’s theorems to (a) (A+B)C​ and (b) ABC+DE​.

(a) Treat A+B as one variable. The bar is over a product, so it becomes a sum:

(A+B)C​=A+B​+C=AB+C

(b) The bar is over a sum, so it becomes a product of the two complemented terms, then each of those is split:

ABC+DE​=(ABC)(DE)=(A+B+C)(D+E)

Example 8: Nonstandard POS to standard POS

Like Lecture 3, Examples 12–13 and 15

Convert X=(A+B)(B+C) to standard POS and find the rows where it’s 0.

  1. Add CC to the first term and AA to the second, then split with Rule 12: A+BB+C​=(A+B+C)(A+B+C)=(A+B+C)(A+B+C)​

Example 13: Design a majority circuit

Like the §2.8 design examples

Design a circuit for three judges (A, B, C) that outputs f=1 when at least two judges vote yes (1). Simplify it.

  1. Truth table: f=1 on rows with two or three 1s: 011,101,110,111.
  2. Standard SOP: f=∑m(3,5,6,7)=ABC+ABC+ABC+ABC
  3. Simplify. Use Rule 5 to copy ABC twice, so it can pair with each of the others: f​=(ABC+ABC)+(ABC+ABC)+(ABC+ABC)=BC(A+A)+AC(B+B)+AB(C+C)=AB+AC+BC​
  4. Circuit: three 2-input ANDs feeding a 3-input OR (or five NAND gates, by Example 10’s method).

Common mistakes

  • Treating Boolean 1+1 like binary: in Boolean algebra (OR), 1+1=1, not 10.
  • Splitting a bar without changing the operation: AB=AB. It’s A+B.
  • Dropping the parentheses when applying DeMorgan: A+BC​=A(B+C), not AB+C.
  • Mixing up the two lists: SOP comes from the 1s (0 → complemented), POS comes from the 0s (1 → complemented).
  • Giving an XOR gate more than two inputs. A multi-input “XOR” is a chain of 2-input XORs (it’s 1 when an odd number of inputs are 1).
  • In NAND/NOR diagrams, connecting a bubble output to a plain input and forgetting that variable now has a bar.
